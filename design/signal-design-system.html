A console for deciding faster than you can think.

When a model scores 400 transactions an hour and a human has eight seconds each, the design problem isn't showing the score — it's stopping the human from either trusting it blindly or ignoring it entirely.

Concept project Self-directed 4 screens designed of 9 specified 3 confidence states Sample data is synthetic

Automation bias

Under time pressure people approve whatever the model suggests without reading. Accuracy looks fine right up until the model drifts.

Alert fatigue

Flag too much and analysts start clearing the queue rather than reviewing it. Volume is itself a design failure.

Asymmetric cost

A false positive freezes a real customer's card at a petrol station. A false negative loses money. The interface has to reflect that these are not equal.

No ground truth

The analyst never finds out whether they were right. There is no feedback loop unless the interface builds one.

01 — Colour

A luminance ramp, not a traffic light

Red, amber and green are out: a meaningful share of analysts cannot separate them, and red produces a panic response in a job where the correct posture is steady attention. The three states run neutral to warm along a luminance ramp — clear is dim and cool, flagged is bright and warm. Photocopy this interface in greyscale and it still reads. One saturated accent is held back entirely for the model's own voice.

Why periwinkle

The accent had to sit outside the warm ramp so it never reads as a state, and outside green and cyan, which analysts read as "good". Periwinkle carries no verdict.

Why focus is white

The obvious focus colour is the accent — but the accent means "the model said this". A focus ring that borrows it would put the model's voice on whatever the analyst happens to be standing on.

02 — Type & density

One family, two sizes that matter

IBM Plex Sans throughout — it holds up at 13px, and its figures are genuinely tabular, which a column of amounts needs. Two sizes carry meaning: 13px for data, where density is the point, and 15px for primary decisions, where a misclick costs a customer their card. Everything else is supporting.

03 — Three states

Uncertain is a state, not a midpoint

The easy mistake is drawing confidence as a continuum and letting "uncertain" be the middle of it — which tells the analyst it is a weaker version of clear. It isn't. It is a different instruction: clear means confirm, flagged means investigate, uncertain means the model is telling you it cannot help. So the encoding is categorical. Continuous bar, broken bar, single heavy mass. No shared geometry between them.

Clear Uncertain Flagged
StateGlyphRow treatmentWhat the analyst should do
Clear Continuous low bar, dim No left rule, no wash Confirm and move. Reading the evidence is optional; it is one keystroke away if you want it.
Uncertain Three separated segments, middle hatched, third faded Dashed left rule, warm wash Slow down. The model has named a tension it cannot resolve — you are the one resolving it.
Flagged Single heavy mass with a leading cap, brightest Solid left rule, warm wash Investigate before rejecting. Confidence that it is fraud is not permission to skip the evidence.

No percentages

"87% confident" is falsely precise and uninterpretable at speed — nobody can act differently at 87 than at 84. Bands force the model to say what it means.

Dashed versus solid

The rule style does the categorical work. Dashed reads as incomplete at a glance and needs no colour, no legend and no training to interpret.

04 — Focus model

The mouse is the fallback

Analysts work this tool almost entirely from the keyboard, so focus is not an accessibility afterthought here — it is the primary interface. 2px solid white, 2px offset, on every focusable element, including table rows. White because the accent belongs to the model. Rows use a roving tabindex, so the queue is one tab stop and the arrow keys move within it.

Tab into these — the ring is the same object everywhere.

05 — Components

Four objects the whole tool is built from

Evidence chip

Every claim the model makes links to the data that produced it. A chip is a claim plus its source, and the source is not optional — an untraceable chip would be the model asserting rather than showing. The left mark carries which side the evidence falls on, so a column of chips reads before it is read.

Device seen 47 times device history → First charge in Türkiye geo log → Travel pattern, 6 countries 12-month history →

Data row

13px, tabular figures, 9px vertical padding — tuned so twelve rows fit a 900px viewport with the header and still clear a comfortable pointer target. State lives in the left rule; the row background carries only the faintest wash, because twelve saturated rows would be a wall.

Confidence indicator

Shown above in Three states. Always paired with its plain-language label — the glyph is a fast read for someone who has seen ten thousand of them, not a replacement for the word.

Decision button group

15px. Every action carries its single-key shortcut in the button itself, so the keyboard path is learned by using the mouse path. Exactly one button may carry the accent: the one the model recommends. When the model has no useful opinion, no button carries it.

Escalate is always present. A binary forces a decision on genuinely ambiguous cases, and a forced decision is where analysts learn to guess.

S1 — Queue

Twelve rows, three states, one keystroke each

The persistent header carries queue depth and pace, because those are the two numbers that change how an analyst works — a queue of 40 and a queue of 400 are different jobs. Pace is shown next to a target rather than alone: a bare average invites optimising for speed.

Why "Review" and not a number

The recommended-action column names an action, not a probability. The analyst's job is to act, so the model's output is phrased as the thing they will do.

Sorting is by age, not risk

Sorting by risk buries uncertain cases under flagged ones and lets the oldest cases rot. Age is the only ordering that doesn't quietly encode a priority the model has not earned.

S2 — High confidence

The happy path, and what it admits it didn't check

Confirmable in one keystroke and readable in about three seconds. The whole decision sits above the fold at 1440×900 — if the analyst has to scroll to decide, the eight-second budget is already gone. The Not checked list is the part that earns trust: a model that names its blind spots is one an analyst can calibrate against.

S3 — Low confidence

Name the tension, then get out of the way

This is the screen the whole system exists for. A low score tells the analyst nothing they can act on. The model has to say what it is torn between, in a sentence a person would say out loud — and then present both sides at genuinely equal weight, in matched columns, so the layout does not quietly pick a winner while claiming neutrality.

The pace cue

A short line and a bar, sitting under the buttons. It does not count down, block, flash or ask to be dismissed — it states a norm and lets the analyst decide. A nag would just teach them to work around it.

Why no recommendation

The accent is the model's voice. Withholding it on a case the model genuinely cannot call is what keeps it meaningful on the cases it can.

S6 — Model degraded

The tool still works. It just claims less.

Almost nobody designs this state, which is why it is worth designing. When the model goes stale or its confidence drops across the board, the interface shifts mode rather than erroring: the accent is suppressed everywhere, the "Recommended" column becomes "Suggested" and its entries go italic and neutral, bulk actions switch off, and a banner says plainly what changed and what to do differently.

Nothing turns warning-coloured. This is not a failure — the queue works exactly as before, and treating it as an error would train analysts to dismiss the banner. Use the toggle on S1 to see the same queue switch between the two modes.

What the banner has to answer

What changed, why, when it resolves, and what the analyst should do differently. Anything less and it is decoration that gets banner-blindness within a shift.

Suppression over substitution

The accent is removed rather than swapped for another colour. Introducing a second accent for degraded mode would mean teaching a new signal at exactly the moment the analyst needs to be reading evidence instead.

Bulk actions off

Bulk clearing is where automation bias becomes measurable harm. It is the first thing to go when the model's confidence drops, and it comes back when the model does.

Still open

What this is, and what it isn't

A concept project. There is no client, no shipped product and no live model. The transactions, merchants and queue statistics on this page are synthetic sample data written to exercise the layout — they are not measurements of anything.

Not yet done — and not to be invented

  • Research. Five interviews with fraud analysts, risk ops and chargeback teams, synthesised into 4–6 themes. No quotes exist yet, so none appear here. Sample size gets stated plainly when they do.
  • Testing. A moderated test, 5 participants, timed, with planted known-fraud cases. Measures: time per case paired with accuracy, override rate, confidence calibration, missed-flag rate. The failures are the findings worth publishing.
  • Five remaining screens. S4 conflicting signals, S5 override capture, S7 bulk-action guardrail, S8 post-decision recall, S9 analyst calibration.
  • Wait states. Evidence streaming in as it resolves, with what is still pending named — specified in the system, not yet drawn.
