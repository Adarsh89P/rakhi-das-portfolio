Healthcare · UX/UI case study

Redesigning diagnostic workflows for faster, simpler healthcare access.

A connected diagnostic platform that brings patients, front desk staff, administrators and home collection teams onto one system — with unified workflows and real-time visibility.

Role
UI/UX Designer
Timeline
Mar – Jul 2025
Platform
Web + Mobile · 5 products
My contribution
Research · UX · UI · Prototyping

01 — Project overview

Five products, five kinds of user, one diagnostic journey.

The problem
Diagnostic workflows were fragmented across disconnected touchpoints, for patients and staff alike.
Users
Patients · Front desk executives · Administrators · Phlebotomists · Home collectors
My role
Sole UI/UX designer — research, workflow mapping, UX strategy, UI and prototyping across all five products.
Outcome
A unified ecosystem that simplifies diagnostics and improves visibility for everyone involved.

02 — The challenge

Diagnostic care involves many people, several systems, and no shared view of any of it.

Diagnostic services are fragmented and time-consuming. Patients struggle with booking tests, tracking reports and navigating multiple touchpoints that don't talk to each other.

Internally, the same journey is stitched together by hand across front desk, admin and home collection — which is where the delays, errors and coordination gaps come from.

The absence of a unified system hurts both the patient experience and operational efficiency, which is what made this a redesign of the whole ecosystem rather than of any single screen.

Who the journey passes through

Patient
Books a test, waits without status, chases a report.
Front desk executive
Registers patients and manages tests and appointments manually.
Administrator
Oversees staff, reports and operations with no single control surface.
Phlebotomist & home collector
Runs sample collection with no task list, route or line back to the system.

01

Fragmented information

Records were scattered across systems and states, so no one could see a full picture of a single test.

02

Multiple manual handoffs

Simple tasks took several steps and re-entry by hand between roles, which is where the errors and the delays entered.

03

Low visibility

Patients couldn't track a test and staff couldn't see what was assigned, in progress, or waiting on them.

03 — Research & insights

I started by following the work, not the screens.

Contextual inquiry with the teams running the service, UX research across the five roles, and a full mapping of the existing workflow. Three findings shaped every decision that followed.

Insight 01

Contextual inquiry

Patients weren't confused by the booking form — they were confused by the silence after it.

The booking flow itself was convoluted, but the sharper problem came after: no real-time updates between booking and result, and reports that were hard to reach once they existed. The journey had no visible state, so patients fell back on phoning the desk.

Design implication

Make status the spine of the patient app. Every test carries one visible state from booking through collection to report, and the report is reachable from that same place.

Insight 02

Workflow mapping

The front desk was absorbing the system's complexity by hand.

Mapping the desk's day showed an outdated workflow where routine tasks took several steps and the same patient details were re-keyed more than once. The manual re-entry was not carelessness — it was the interface asking for it, and that is where errors and queue time came from.

Design implication

Collapse registration into one quick path, and put tests and appointments on a single screen so the desk stops navigating between systems to serve one patient.

Insight 03

UX research across roles

Every role could see its own step, and no role could see the operation.

Administrators had no centralised control over staff, reports and operations. Home collectors worked without an assigned task list or a planned route, and had no channel back into the system. Each role was locally functional and globally blind, so coordination happened over phone calls.

Design implication

Design one connected system, not five apps. Admin gets a single control surface, collectors get assigned tasks with details and an optimised route, and all of them read and write the same records the desk touches.

04 — UX strategy

An MVP route: test the core of the journey first, then widen it.

Five products at once is a way to get five products wrong. The strategy was to establish the journey end to end at low fidelity, validate it with the people who run the service, and only then design each role's interface against a shape everyone had already agreed on.

05 — Design evolution

Four phases, and a deliberate refusal to draw screens early.

The methodology below is the one the project actually ran on. The first half is entirely non-visual — inquiry, research, workflow mapping, documented findings — because the problem here was structural, and a high-fidelity screen drawn before the structure is settled is just an expensive guess.

Discover

Contextual inquiry, UX research and mapping the existing user workflow — establishing what the service actually does before proposing what it should do.

Define

Documenting findings and recommendations, then prioritising the user journey so the MVP had a defensible scope rather than a wish list.

Ideation

Exploring directions through lo-fi designs first, and only then building high-fidelity prototypes — so the cheap explorations absorbed the wrong turns.

Implementation

Testing and feasibility with the teams who would run it, then release and monitoring impact. Feasibility sat before release deliberately: this system has to work on the floor.

06 — Final solution

Five interfaces, designed as one system.

Each product below solves its role's specific problem, but they share one record, one status vocabulary and one component set — which is what turns five apps into an ecosystem.

01

Patient app

Booking a test and following it to the report, without phoning anyone.

Problem
A confusing booking flow, no real-time updates, and reports that were hard to access.
Design decision
Reduce booking to a few clear steps, then keep the same test visible afterwards with a live status and its report attached to it.
Why it works
The thing the patient booked and the thing they are waiting on are the same object on screen, so there is nothing to go looking for and no reason to call the desk for a status.

1 Search and quick actions sit above the fold — the two things a returning patient came for.

2 Upcoming appointments are on the home screen, not behind a tab, so status needs no navigation.

3 Availability is shown at the point of choosing, so the flow has no dead ends.

4 Date and slot are one step, sized for one-handed use.

02

Front desk dashboard

Registering a patient and managing their tests from a single screen.

Problem
A confusing, outdated workflow where simple tasks took multiple steps and patient handling was slow and error-prone.
Design decision
Make registration a quick single path, and bring test and appointment management onto the same surface as the patient record.
Why it works
The desk serves a queue, so its cost is measured in steps per patient. Removing the navigation between systems removes both the wait and the re-entry that caused the errors.

1 Patient record and booking actions occupy one view — no context switch mid-task.

2 Test and appointment management sit where the record already is.

3 Dense table layout, because this is an operational tool used all day and scanning beats scrolling.

03

Admin dashboard

One place to run staff, reports, users and operations.

Problem
No centralised control. Managing staff, reports and day-to-day operations meant working across disconnected places.
Design decision
Give the administrator complete system control in one surface — users, reports and analytics together rather than as separate destinations.
Why it works
An administrator's job is comparison: this team against that one, today against last week. Analytics next to the records they summarise is what makes a decision possible without exporting anything.

1 Overview metrics lead, so the state of the operation is the first thing read.

2 User and staff management is a peer of reporting, not a settings page.

3 Charts sit beside the underlying records they summarise.

04

Home collector app

Knowing what to collect, where, in what order — on a phone, in the field.

Problem
No proper task tracking, inefficient routing and scheduling, and communication gaps back to the rest of the team.
Design decision
Assign tasks explicitly, with the visit details attached, and give the day an optimised order rather than a list to interpret.
Why it works
This is the only role using the system while moving, one-handed, often outdoors. A task that already knows its own address, patient and sample removes every decision that would otherwise need a phone call.

1 Assigned tasks carry their own details — no cross-referencing another system.

2 The visit order is given, not derived by the collector.

3 Status updates flow back into the same records the desk and admin read.

05

Website

The public front door: finding a test and booking it, and trusting the place you're booking with.

Problem
An outdated interface with poor navigation, which cost the service both engagement and credibility.
Design decision
Rebuild around test discovery and booking as the primary path, in a clean, current visual language shared with the rest of the system.
Why it works
In healthcare the interface is part of the credibility judgement. A site that looks maintained and gets a first-time visitor to the thing they came for is doing trust work and conversion work with the same move.

1 Test discovery and booking is the primary path, not a secondary link.

2 Navigation is reduced to the destinations a first-time visitor actually needs.

3 The same components and colour system as the internal tools, so the brand holds across the whole journey.

07 — Responsive experience

One system, three postures.

Screen size here is a proxy for the work being done. The desk and the administrator sit down with the whole operation in front of them; the collector is standing in a doorway with one hand free. The same records, the same status vocabulary — a different amount of it, chosen deliberately rather than by breakpoint.

Desktop

Front desk · Admin
What it carries
Information-dense operational workflow: records, tables, analytics and management side by side.
What stays
The full record and every status.
Why
These roles compare and cross-reference all day. Density is the feature — hiding data behind navigation would cost them steps per patient.

Tablet

Shared floor use
What it carries
The same workflow with the supporting columns folded away — the primary task keeps the full width.
What changes
Side panels stack under the main view; touch targets grow to finger size.
Why
A tablet on the floor is used standing and at arm's length. Reach and target size become the constraint, not screen area.

Mobile

Patient · Home collector
What it carries
A focused, task-based experience: one test with its status, or one visit with its details.
What changes
Everything not needed for the current task is removed rather than collapsed.
Why
Both roles are mobile by nature — a patient checking in passing, a collector between visits. Neither is browsing, so a scaled-down dashboard would be the wrong answer even if it fitted.

System & accessibility decisions

A shared component set

Tables, cards, form fields, navigation and status indicators are defined once and reused across all five products, so a pattern a front desk executive learns transfers to the admin dashboard unchanged.

Status is never colour alone

Every state carries a label as well as a colour. In a diagnostic workflow a misread status has clinical consequences, and roughly one in twelve men cannot separate the usual red/green pairing.

Built to scale

The system was designed to absorb new test types, roles and screens without re-authoring — which is what let five products ship against one visual language rather than five.

Sized for real conditions

Touch targets on the collector and patient apps are sized for one-handed outdoor use; the desk and admin tools favour scanning density for seated, all-day work. Typography and contrast follow the same split.

08 — The outcome

Boosts patient satisfaction and cuts documentation workload.

  1. 01

    A unified ecosystem serving all five kinds of user from one connected system.

  2. 02

    Reduced manual work and the errors that came with re-entering the same data.

  3. 03

    An improved patient experience, with a test that can be followed from booking to report.

  4. 04

    A scalable design system that holds across web and mobile as the service grows.

What I learned

Designing for healthcare taught me that reducing complexity isn't about removing information — it's about delivering the right information, at the right time, to the right person.

The hardest part of this project was not any single interface. It was resisting the urge to design screens before the workflow underneath them was settled, when five different roles each had a confident opinion about what their screen should show.
